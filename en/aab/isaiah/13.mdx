## Footnotes

<ol className="fnl">
<li id="fn-a"><a href="#fnref-a" className="fnb">a</a> <span className="fnr">13:6</span> Hebrew Shaddai</li>
<li id="fn-b"><a href="#fnref-b" className="fnb">b</a> <span className="fnr">13:21</span> Heb. אֹחִים (ohim), 'keening ones' or 'keening creatures.' Appears only here in the canon. The root points to mournful howling. The LXX renders as ἦχος ('sound/resonance') rather than a named creature. Revelation 18:2 reads Babylon's desolation as "a lair for demons and a haunt for every unclean spirit," drawing directly on Isaiah 13 and 34; the ohim fill those houses as part of that company.</li>
<li id="fn-c"><a href="#fnref-c" className="fnb">c</a> <span className="fnr">13:21</span> Heb. בְּנוֹת יַעֲנָה (benot ya'anah). Most dictionaries and English translations identify it as "daughters of the ostrich" or simply "ostriches." The LXX renders as σειρῆνες (seirēnes): supernatural beings with a woman's head and a bird's body whose wailing voices lured the living to their deaths. Micah 1:8 uses the term as a figure of mournful wailing; Job 30:29 pairs it with תַּנִּים as a figure of desolation. Revelation 18:2 requires these ruins to be populated by hostile spiritual beings.</li>
<li id="fn-d"><a href="#fnref-d" className="fnb">d</a> <span className="fnr">13:21</span> Heb. שְׂעִירִים (se'irim), 'hairy ones' or 'goat-beings.' Leviticus 17:7 prohibits Israel from sacrificing to the se'irim; 2 Chronicles 11:15 records Jeroboam appointing priests for them alongside the golden calves. The LXX renders it δαιμόνια (daimonia), ‘demons,’ and the Aramaic Targums render it ‘demons’ (shedim) here and at Lev 17:7 and Isa 34:14; Revelation 18:2 uses the same Greek word of fallen Babylon.</li>
<li id="fn-e"><a href="#fnref-e" className="fnb">e</a> <span className="fnr">13:22</span> Heb. iyyim, ‘howlers,’ found only in these oracles of desolation (Isa 13:22; 34:14; Jer 50:39). The word’s origin is uncertain; ‘howlers’ follows the likeliest link, to a word for a howling dog. What the passages make plain is the company they keep. Wherever God’s judgment empties a city, the iyyim move in with the desert creatures (tsiyyim), the goat demons (se’irim), and Lilith (Isa 34:14), and the Greek translators rendered them onokentauroi, ‘donkey-centaurs,’ creatures neither human nor beast. Revelation sees fallen Babylon become ‘a lair for demons and a haunt for every unclean spirit’ (Rev 18:2).</li>
<li id="fn-f"><a href="#fnref-f" className="fnb">f</a> <span className="fnr">13:22</span> Heb. תַּנִּים / תַּנִּין (tannim/tannin), 'sea-dragon' or 'chaos-creature.' Canonical range runs from chaos-dragon (Ps 74:13; Isa 27:1; 51:9, where tannin parallels Rahab) to the devouring sea-beast of creation (Gen 1:21; Job 7:12). Jeremiah 51:34 applies the word directly to Nebuchadnezzar: "he has swallowed me like a dragon." The LXX often renders it δράκοντες, ‘dragons’ (Jer 9:11; Mic 1:8); the KJV renders 'dragons.' Most modern translations default to 'jackals,' driven by naturalistic assumptions rather than the canonical weight of the term.</li>
</ol>
