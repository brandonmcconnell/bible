## Footnotes

<ol className="fnl">
<li id="fn-a"><a href="#fnref-a" className="fnb">a</a> <span className="fnr">9:20</span> Or was the first</li>
<li id="fn-b"><a href="#fnref-b" className="fnb">b</a> <span className="fnr">9:21</span> Heb. oholoh. The word ends in the letter he, which ordinarily marks ‘her,’ so the consonants read ‘her tent,’ and in this household that woman is Noah’s wife. The vowel marks that make it ‘his’ were added by scribes centuries after the consonants were fixed, and without them the word can be read either way. Most English translations print ‘his tent,’ following those later marks. The AAB reads ‘her’ because that reading is equally defensible in the Hebrew and explains far more: uncovering a father’s nakedness is Torah’s language for relations with his wife (Lev 18:8; 20:11), and it accounts for the curse falling on Canaan rather than on Ham (Gen 9:25).</li>
<li id="fn-c"><a href="#fnref-c" className="fnb">c</a> <span className="fnr">9:22</span> ‘Saw his father’s nakedness’ carries a legal sense in the Torah, where to uncover a father’s nakedness means relations with his wife (Lev 18:8; 20:11). Read together with ‘her tent’ in verse 21, the account points to an act against Noah’s wife, told with deliberate restraint. This also accounts for the curse falling on Canaan rather than on Ham (Gen 9:25), Canaan being understood as born of it.</li>
<li id="fn-d"><a href="#fnref-d" className="fnb">d</a> <span className="fnr">9:27</span> Japheth sounds like the Hebrew for expand.</li>
