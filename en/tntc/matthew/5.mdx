## About respecting others, especially ‘brothers’

<div className="bt">
<div className="bp">

<span className="v" id="v22" data-v="22" data-xref="1 John 3:14–15; 1 John 4:20–21; 1 John 2:9"> But I say to you that whoever gets angry with his brother without cause will be liable to the judgment.<sup className="fn"><a href="#fn-j" id="fnref-j" data-fn="God hates injustice and will judge it. NIV, NASB and LB favor us with a footnote informing us that “some manuscripts” add ‘without cause’—by “some” they mean 98% of them!! More serious, the shorter text has the effect of forbidding anger, which would contradict other Scriptures (Ephesians 4:26, Psalm 4:4) and the Lord's own example (Mark 3:5).">j</a></sup>And whoever says to his brother, ‘Numbskull!’ will be liable to the council.<sup className="fn"><a href="#fn-k" id="fnref-k" data-fn="The Greek text has a transliteration of the Aramaic term, raka. However, note that the Lord is talking about saying this to a brother. He Himself applied this term to the scribes and Pharisees in chapter 23. Verses 22-24 deal with how we treat ‘brothers’. Consider James 4:11-12: “Brothers, do not speak evil of one another. Because the one speaking against a brother and judging his brother speaks against a law and judges a law. So if you judge a law you are not a law-doer but a judge. The Lawgiver and Judge is One, the One who is able to save and to destroy. So who are you (sg) to be judging someone who is different?” I was surprised to find the Greek ετερος here, which usually refers to a different kind. I personally do not enjoy dealing with ‘brothers’ who are too different; I would rather question whether they are really ‘brothers’ at all! But James tells me not to do that. Each person is different (background, experiences, personality, training) and we must recognize that God can and will deal differently with different people. He uses one ‘law’ with me, another ‘law’ with you, and so on. A law is a set of rules or demands, so when I judge a brother I am questioning the way (‘law’) that God is working on him. As He is both Lawgiver and Judge, I will have to answer to Him for how I judged my ‘brothers’. (For ‘different one’ the eclectic text currently in vogue has ‘neighbor’, following some 12% of the Greek manuscripts [as in NIV, TEV, LB, NASB, etc.].)">l</a></sup></span>
<span className="v" id="v23" data-v="23" data-xref="Mark 11:25; Leviticus 6:2–6; Matthew 5:24"> Therefore, if you bring your gift to the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you,<sup className="fn"><a href="#fn-m" id="fnref-m" data-fn="Verse 23 is linked to 22; insult or injury is involved—an overt action, an unpleasant scene that will presumably be alive in your memory.">m</a></sup></span>

<span className="v" id="v26" data-v="26" data-xref="Matthew 18:34; Luke 12:59; 2 Thessalonians 1:9"> Assuredly I say to you, you will by no means get out of there until you have paid the last penny.<sup className="fn"><a href="#fn-o" id="fnref-o" data-fn="Verses 25-26 refer to financial debt, through property damage, a loan, etc. There is no doubt about the fact of the debt, and the time limit has expired; and you are on the way to court—at that point the only way out is to humble yourself and throw yourself on the mercy of the creditor.">o</a></sup></span>

Less than 2% of the Greek manuscripts, of inferior quality, omit “without cause” (as in NIV, NASB, LB, TEV, etc.). NIV, NASB and LB favor us with a footnote informing us that “some manuscripts” add ‘without cause’—by “some” they mean 98% of them!! More serious, the shorter text has the effect of forbidding anger, which would contradict other Scriptures (Ephesians 4:26, Psalm 4:4) and the Lord's own example (Mark 3:5).</li>
<li id="fn-k"><a href="#fnref-k" className="fnb">k</a> <span className="fnr">5:22</span> The Greek text has a transliteration of the Aramaic term, raka. The idea seems to be that this was an offensive epithet that could lead to a court case, for defamation of character (or whatever).</li>
<li id="fn-l"><a href="#fnref-l" className="fnb">l</a> <span className="fnr">5:22</span> This presumably involves an offense against God, denigrating His image, disparaging His work. However, note that the Lord is talking about saying this to a brother. He Himself applied this term to the scribes and Pharisees in chapter 23. Verses 22-24 deal with how we treat ‘brothers’. Consider James 4:11-12: “Brothers, do not speak evil of one another. Because the one speaking against a brother and judging his brother speaks against a law and judges a law. So if you judge a law you are not a law-doer but a judge. The Lawgiver and Judge is One, the One who is able to save and to destroy. So who are you (sg) to be judging someone who is different?” I was surprised to find the Greek ετερος here, which usually refers to a different kind. I personally do not enjoy dealing with ‘brothers’ who are too different; I would rather question whether they are really ‘brothers’ at all! But James tells me not to do that. Each person is different (background, experiences, personality, training) and we must recognize that God can and will deal differently with different people. He uses one ‘law’ with me, another ‘law’ with you, and so on. A law is a set of rules or demands, so when I judge a brother I am questioning the way (‘law’) that God is working on him. As He is both Lawgiver and Judge, I will have to answer to Him for how I judged my ‘brothers’. (For ‘different one’ the eclectic text currently in vogue has ‘neighbor’, following some 12% of the Greek manuscripts &#91;as in NIV, TEV, LB, NASB, etc.&#93;.)</li>
<li id="fn-m"><a href="#fnref-m" className="fnb">m</a> <span className="fnr">5:23</span> Verse 23 is linked to 22; insult or injury is involved—an overt action, an unpleasant scene that will presumably be alive in your memory.</li>
<li id="fn-n"><a href="#fnref-n" className="fnb">n</a> <span className="fnr">5:24</span> Verses 23-24 refer to moral debt, through abusive language.</li>
<li id="fn-o"><a href="#fnref-o" className="fnb">o</a> <span className="fnr">5:26</span> Verses 25-26 refer to financial debt, through property damage, a loan, etc. There is no doubt about the fact of the debt, and the time limit has expired; and you are on the way to court—at that point the only way out is to humble yourself and throw yourself on the mercy of the creditor.</li>
<li id="fn-p"><a href="#fnref-p" className="fnb">p</a> <span className="fnr">5:27</span> Perhaps 10% of the Greek manuscripts add ‘to those of old’ (as in AV and NKJV).</li>
<li id="fn-q"><a href="#fnref-q" className="fnb">q</a> <span className="fnr">5:28</span> If he had the courage and the opportunity, he would do it.</li>
<li id="fn-r"><a href="#fnref-r" className="fnb">r</a> <span className="fnr">5:29</span> This is literal, but mostly theoretical, since by the time people realize they are in trouble it is usually too late; they are already trapped. Perhaps this is why Hebrews 3:13 tells us to “exhort one another daily… lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin”. Sometimes others see that we are in trouble before we do.</li>
<li id="fn-s"><a href="#fnref-s" className="fnb">s</a> <span className="fnr">5:32</span> Presumably a reference to premarital sex, where a groom discovers that his bride is no longer a virgin (like the situation that Joseph faced in 1:18-19). See Matthew 19:3-12.</li>
<li id="fn-t"><a href="#fnref-t" className="fnb">t</a> <span className="fnr">5:32</span> In that culture such a woman would look for another man as a matter of physical survival.</li>
<li id="fn-u"><a href="#fnref-u" className="fnb">u</a> <span className="fnr">5:37</span> Presumably this precludes any and all exaggeration, putting a ‘spin’ on things, etc.</li>
<li id="fn-v"><a href="#fnref-v" className="fnb">v</a> <span className="fnr">5:37</span> Do we really believe this? If not, we had better go back to the drawing board.</li>
<li id="fn-w"><a href="#fnref-w" className="fnb">w</a> <span className="fnr">5:38</span> The reference is to Exodus 21:24, but verse 22 makes clear that it is “as the judges determine”—we are not to take the law into our own hands.</li>
<li id="fn-x"><a href="#fnref-x" className="fnb">x</a> <span className="fnr">5:39</span> Do not use physical violence to respond to physical violence—but we can use our spiritual authority. Animals must resort to force, but Adam was given dominion over the animals, which presumably was not exercised with physical force—we should not lower ourselves to the animal level; but Satan tries to trick us into doing so. If someone lashes out in anger, turning the other cheek should shame him.</li>
<li id="fn-y"><a href="#fnref-y" className="fnb">y</a> <span className="fnr">5:40</span> Do not use legal action to respond to legal action—but we can use our spiritual authority. If they are resorting to legal action they presumably do not want to talk it over, they are trying to impose something on us; so turning the other cheek could shame them.</li>
<li id="fn-z"><a href="#fnref-z" className="fnb">z</a> <span className="fnr">5:41</span> A Roman imposition on subjugated peoples—any Roman soldier could compel a local non-Roman citizen to carry his pack one mile. A cheerful response to this humiliating situation could at least deprive the soldier of sadistic satisfaction, and maybe even shame him into carrying his own pack.</li>
<li id="fn-aa"><a href="#fnref-aa" className="fnb">aa</a> <span className="fnr">5:42</span> Presumably this does not refer to professional beggars (2 Thessalonians 3:10), but to someone overtaken by adversity who needs temporary help, someone you know who is part of the community.</li>
<li id="fn-ab"><a href="#fnref-ab" className="fnb">ab</a> <span className="fnr">5:43</span> Leviticus 19:18 says to “love your neighbor”, but not “hate your enemy”. The scribes had added an unwarranted inference.</li>
<li id="fn-ac"><a href="#fnref-ac" className="fnb">ac</a> <span className="fnr">5:44</span> Less than 1% of the Greek manuscripts, of inferior quality, offer a truncated form of this verse (as in NIV, NASB, LB, TEV, etc.).</li>
<li id="fn-ad"><a href="#fnref-ad" className="fnb">ad</a> <span className="fnr">5:47</span> Perhaps 4% of the Greek manuscripts have ‘brothers’, as in many versions.</li>
<li id="fn-ae"><a href="#fnref-ae" className="fnb">ae</a> <span className="fnr">5:47</span> The reference to “reward” and “extra” suggests that the procedures previously enjoined are demonstrations of practical righteousness—we are to be examples, rise above the level of the surrounding society. Tax collectors were Jews, part of the community, but despised as traitors.</li>
<li id="fn-af"><a href="#fnref-af" className="fnb">af</a> <span className="fnr">5:48</span> “Perfect”—the Father is our point of reference; we are to be like He is. A standard is a standard; it is not invalidated just because we may feel that it is unattainable. Comparing this passage with texts like Deuteronomy 7:10, “He repays those who hate Him to their face”, and Psalm 5:5-6, “You hate all workers of iniquity”, I take it that we must distinguish between personal enemies (those who oppose us for personal reasons) and enemies of God and His truth. To be like the Father we also must hate workers of iniquity (because of the consequences to others).</li>
</ol>
