<div className="cmv" id="v1" data-v="1">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:1](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v1)</div>
<p>32:1 Listen, O heavens. . . . Hear, O earth: Moses appealed to the witnesses of the covenant to note Israel’s confession and commitment as well as its anticipated disobedience and disloyalty (see 30:19).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v2" data-v="2">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:2](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v2)</div>
<p>32:2 like rain . . . like dew: This simile shows the refreshing gentleness of Moses’ teaching. It should produce the fruit of obedience in the hearts of God’s people.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v4" data-v="4">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:4](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v4)</div>
<p>32:4 the Rock: This metaphor represents the Lord as strong, stable, and reliable; he is more than capable of providing righteousness and justice (see 32:15, 18, 30; Hab 1:12).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v7" data-v="7">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:7](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v7)</div>
<p>32:7 Ask your father: History instructs those who are willing to learn from it. In the wisdom tradition of the Old Testament, the father was given the task of teaching his children the lessons that had previously been transmitted to him (see, e.g., Prov 1:8; 2:1; 3:1).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v8" data-v="8">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:8](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v8)</div>
<p>32:8 God is sovereign over and concerned with all nations and has allotted their land in accord with his own purposes (see Ps 74:17; Acts 17:26). • the Most High (Hebrew ‘Elyon): Abraham learned this name for God when he encountered Melchizedek (Gen 14:19). This ancient name describes God’s universal sovereignty. • assigned lands: One evidence of God’s sovereignty was his distribution of lands to the nations (see Gen 10).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v9" data-v="9">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:9](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v9)</div>
<p>32:9 his special possession (literally his inheritance): God declared Israel to be his son, whom he redeemed from bondage in Egypt (see Exod 4:22-23; 13:15; Deut 7:6).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v14" data-v="14">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:14](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v14)</div>
<p>32:14 rams from Bashan, and goats: This high plateau east of the Sea of Galilee was famous for such livestock (see study note on 1:4; see also Ps 22:12; Ezek 39:18; Amos 4:1).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v15" data-v="15">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:15](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v15)</div>
<p>32:15 Israel: Hebrew Jeshurun. This name, which appears to be derived from a verb meaning “to be upright,” demonstrates the Lord’s strong affection for his people (see 33:5, 26; Isa 44:2). • the Rock: This metaphor describes God’s strength and reliability, especially regarding salvation (see Deut 32:4).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v16" data-v="16">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:16](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v16)</div>
<p>32:16 his jealousy: See study note on 4:24. Just as a husband is properly jealous when another man tries to draw his wife away (Prov 6:34), God was jealous when Israel worshiped other gods.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v17" data-v="17">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:17](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v17)</div>
<p>32:17 demons: In the ancient Near East, the gods were thought to protect individuals and places from harm, but worshiping these pagan deities actually amounted to worshiping evil spirits (cp. 1 Cor 10:20-21; Rev 9:20). • New gods were false deities that Israel had never before encountered.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v18" data-v="18">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:18](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v18)</div>
<p>32:18 the Rock: This expression draws attention to God’s reliability as Father (see 32:4, 9).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v20" data-v="20">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:20](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v20)</div>
<p>32:20 The phrase without integrity indicates disloyalty to the covenant. Israel had promised to be true to the Lord but had consistently been unfaithful (see Exod 19:8).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v21" data-v="21">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:21](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v21)</div>
<p>32:21 roused my jealousy: The Lord was not petulant because of attention paid to false gods. Rather, Israel had broken their covenant with him by worshiping other gods, demonstrating their disdain for him and his sovereignty (see 32:16). • people who are not even a people: God’s purpose for Israel was not just to redeem them, but through them to redeem all nations of the earth. In Israel’s eyes, other peoples were worthless, but through God’s attention to other nations, Israel would be drawn back to obedience (see Hos 1:9; 2:23; Rom 9:25; 10:19).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v22" data-v="22">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:22](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v22)</div>
<p>32:22 The realm of the dead was thought to be in the depths beneath the earth, and the entrance to that world was the grave. God is omnipresent, and his judgment reaches the most inaccessible places (see Ps 139:7-12).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v27" data-v="27">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:27](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v27)</div>
<p>32:27 I feared: God is never terrified or intimidated. This fear is concern that Israel’s enemies would attribute their success to their own efforts rather than to God’s sovereign judgment of his people (see Exod 32:11-14).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v29" data-v="29">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:29](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v29)</div>
<p>32:29 their fate (literally their latter end): Israel’s future had not been predetermined; this statement expresses how things would turn out if Israel continued on its rebellious course.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v30" data-v="30">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:30](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v30)</div>
<p>32:30 How could one person chase a thousand: When God gives his people over to destruction, they are vulnerable against all odds. • their Rock: Here the metaphor is used ironically. The very one upon whom Israel should stand as the source of their strength will desert them because of their own wickedness and unfaithfulness (see 32:4).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v31" data-v="31">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:31](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v31)</div>
<p>32:31 the rock of our enemies: In this sarcastic comparison between the pagan gods and the Lord God of Israel, the false deities come up woefully short.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v32" data-v="32">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:32](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v32)</div>
<p>32:32 Sodom . . . Gomorrah: These cities were the epitome of evil (see Gen 18:16–19:38). Pagan perversity and godlessness found their inspiration and source in these cities, and because they were unrepentant, they were utterly destroyed (see Deut 29:23).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v34" data-v="34">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:34](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v34)</div>
<p>32:34 Metaphorically, my treasury is the place where God, as Divine Warrior, lays up his implements of war and judgment until the time comes for them to be put to use (see 1:30).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v36" data-v="36">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:36](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v36)</div>
<p>32:36 will change his mind about: When speaking of God, the Old Testament frequently uses figurative language (see study note on 8:2). Human beings cannot understand God’s mind (Isa 55:8), so God uses human language and gives himself human physical characteristics and feelings. Although the Bible frequently speaks of God’s eyes, arms, hands, and feet, he is invisible and has no body; these metaphors describe his activity. Similarly, when the Bible says that God fears (Deut 32:27) or changes his mind, it expresses how God’s actions appear to humans.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v37" data-v="37">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:37](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v37)</div>
<p>32:37 the rocks: This sarcastic metaphor concerns the impotence of pagan gods (see 32:31).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v38" data-v="38">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:38](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v38)</div>
<p>32:38 ate the fat . . . drank the wine: Most polytheistic religious systems that practice sacrifice include the notion that the gods find nourishment in the sacrifices made to them. Israel fell victim to this superstitious belief for a time before the Lord graciously taught them otherwise and restored them to himself (32:36).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v40" data-v="40">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:40](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v40)</div>
<p>32:40 I raise my hand to heaven: This anthropomorphism (see study notes on 4:34 and 8:2) asserts God’s solemn intention to keep his covenant promises to deliver his people from their enemies. When God makes an oath, he is not bound to some course of action in the event that he proves undependable. Rather, the statement emphasizes the reliability of God’s promises above and beyond his character alone (see Num 14:21, 28; Isa 49:18; Ezek 20:5-6).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v42" data-v="42">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:42](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v42)</div>
<p>32:42 The phrase arrows drunk with blood combines personification and hyperbole to show the extent of the carnage that will result from God’s judgment of the nations.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v47" data-v="47">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:47](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v47)</div>
<p>32:47 they are your life: Obeying the Lord’s words brings life (see 8:3; 30:20). • long life: This promise does not guarantee individual longevity and certainly not immortality; it is about Israel’s long tenure in the Land of Promise.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v49" data-v="49">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:49](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v49)</div>
<p>32:49 The mountains east of the river are a range of mountains overlooking the Jordan Valley. Mount Nebo, a prominent peak in this range, was the point from which Moses could view Canaan to the west and was also where he died (32:50; see 3:17).</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v50" data-v="50">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:50](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v50)</div>
<p>32:50 The location of Mount Hor is uncertain, but the traditional location (Jebel Haroun) is within sight of the Nabatean city of Petra (see Num 20:22-29). • The phrase joined his ancestors is a euphemism for burial.</p>
</div>

<div className="cmv" id="v51" data-v="51">
<div className="cmv-head">[Deuteronomy 32:51](/en/kjv/deuteronomy/32#v51)</div>
<p>32:51 you betrayed me: During the wilderness sojourn, Moses dishonored the Lord by striking a water-producing rock. This fit of impatient anger disqualified Moses from entering the Promised Land (Num 20:1-13; see Deut 1:37; 3:26-27).</p>
</div>

</div>
